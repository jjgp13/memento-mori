# Nudging toward a better life: what behavior science says

How Memento Mori can get people to actually reach out, move, and try new things — without becoming one more
app that nags. Researched October 2026. Companion to [science.md](science.md) (the *what*); this is the *how*.

> Same caveat as the science brief: most effects here are modest, many fade, and experts are bad at
> predicting which nudge will work (see §1e). Treat these as starting points to try, not guarantees.

---

## 1. What the evidence says works

### a. Make it a plan, not a wish: "if-then" plans
Turning "I should call Mom more" into **"When I finish Sunday lunch, I'll call Mom"** — a specific cue plus a
specific action — is one of the most robust findings in behavior change. Across 94 tests, these
*implementation intentions* had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (d ≈ 0.65); a 2024 update covers
642 tests. They work best when the goal already matters to the person — which, in this app, it does.

**For us:** each person they love and each habit can have a little plan: *when* and *what*. Reminders then
fire at *their* moment, not ours.

### b. Prompts help — right away, and less over time
- **HeartSteps** (micro-randomized trial, 44 adults, 6 weeks): a walking suggestion at a well-chosen moment
  raised steps in the next 30 minutes by **66% at first**, fading to about **14% on average** by the end.
- A workplace well-being app (1,255 users, 89 days): a push notification made people **3.9% more likely**
  to engage that day; the best moment in an exploratory analysis was **12:30 on weekends** (+11.8%).

**For us:** prompts work, but each one spends a little attention. Few, well-timed, varied, and tied to
something the person chose. Our 1–2 per week is the right instinct.

### c. Habits take weeks to months
In the best-known study, a new daily habit took a **median of 66 days** to feel automatic, with a huge range
(18 to 254 days; small sample, not yet replicated). It's not 21 days.

**For us:** design for the long haul (2–3 months), anchor new habits to existing cues ("after dinner"), and
make a missed day harmless.

### d. Fresh starts are real
Gym visits, "diet" searches and goal commitments all rise after **temporal landmarks**: a new week, a new
month, a birthday, a holiday. People feel they can leave their old self behind.

**For us:** offer the "one thing for this week/month" prompt on Mondays, the 1st, and especially **birthdays**
(a natural "here's your year" moment for a mortality app).

### e. Reward coming back, not perfection
The largest test of exercise nudges so far (**54 interventions, 61,293 gym members**, *Nature* 2021):
- 45% of the ideas increased gym visits, by **9–27%**.
- The best one rewarded **returning after a missed workout**.
- Only **8%** still showed an effect after the 4-week program ended.
- Expert forecasters **couldn't predict** which ideas would work.

**For us:** no streaks that break and shame. Welcome people back warmly. And keep testing ideas rather than
trusting our intuition — ideally with opt-in, privacy-preserving feedback (we don't track people).

### f. Seeing your own progress helps
Across 122 evaluations of activity and diet programs (44,747 people), programs that included
**self-monitoring plus at least one other self-regulation technique** (goals, feedback, plans) did better
(effect 0.42 vs 0.26).

**For us:** "We talked" logs, the monthly tally, steps over time — and a short weekly look back.

### g. Death reminders motivate — when paired with something doable
This is the core of Memento Mori, so it matters most:
- **Terror Management Health Model:** *conscious* thoughts about death push people toward health-protective
  behavior. People reminded of mortality while exercising reported **more exercise two weeks later**;
  smokers made more attempts to quit.
- **Fear appeals** (127 articles, 27,372 people): they work (d ≈ 0.29), and work **better when the message
  includes efficacy** — a clear, doable action — and for one-time actions. Little evidence of backfiring.
- The flip side from the same research: when people feel they *can't* act, or the behavior threatens their
  self-image, death reminders can backfire.

**For us:** never show time running out without **one concrete, doable thing to do now** — "send this
message", "walk 10 minutes after dinner". That's the "scarcity + agency" rule from CLAUDE.md, now with
evidence behind it.

### h. People underestimate how much reaching out means
- Across experiments with **5,900+ people**, those who reached out to a friend **underestimated how much it
  was appreciated** — most of all when the contact was a surprise or the tie had weakened.
- People writing gratitude letters underestimated how happy and surprised recipients would be, and
  overestimated how awkward it would feel — and that misjudgment stopped them from writing.

**For us:** say it right next to the Message button: *"People appreciate being reached out to more than we
expect — especially after a while."* And keep the pre-written openers: they remove the "what do I say?"
barrier.

### i. "Could", not "should"
Messages that support autonomy reduce defiance compared with controlling ones (25,718 people in 89
countries); evidence on changing behavior directly is mixed. Self-determination-theory programs have modest
but real effects on health.

**For us:** gentle, choice-giving language. Never guilt, never "you failed".

### j. Tiny actions with real payoffs
Light walking breaks of a few minutes, including after meals, lowered blood sugar about **17%** compared with
sitting (meta-analysis of 7 studies).

**For us:** "a 5-minute walk after dinner" is a nudge that's small enough to do and backed by evidence.

### k. Your own numbers, shown well
- Smokers told their **"lung age"** were about **twice as likely to quit** within a year (13.6% vs 6.4%,
  randomized trial of 561 smokers). Personal, concrete numbers beat abstract risk.
- But across trials, communicating risk on its own changes behavior **only a little**. It works when it comes
  with a way to act (§1a, §1g).
- Repeated prompts wear off (§1b), so one fresh count a day beats a wall of numbers.
- Sleep is not lost time: 7–8 hours is linked to a longer life. Show it as rest, never as waste.

**For us:** the Life calendar's "What if…" lets people try a change and watch the bronze weeks appear, then
offers a small plan for it right there. Today shows one rotating count, most often a person or thing they
love, with the next step under it. The "Awake / Off phone" view counts sleep as time well spent.

## 2. What this means for Memento Mori

### Plans
- For each person they love: **"When will you reach out?"** — day, time, or an anchor ("Sunday after lunch").
- For habits: one small plan at a time ("after dinner, a 10-minute walk").
- Suggested defaults, always editable; never more than one new plan per week.

### Reminders (1–2 a week by default, the person chooses)
- **Local notifications** scheduled on the phone: no server, no account, nothing leaves the device.
- Each reminder is **one person or one action**, specific, with the next step one tap away:
  "Sunday, 12:30 · About 90 visits left with Mom. Call her today?" → opens the app on Mom, Call/Message ready.
- Default slot: **weekend around midday** (best engagement in §1b), or their chosen plan time.
- Rotate the type — reach out, move, reflect — weighted toward their values.
- Fresh-start moments: Monday "one thing this week", 1st of the month, **birthday look-back**.

### Tone rules
- Every "time left" is paired with a doable action (§1g). That includes healthy years: the "Strong and
  independent" card on Life always ends with one change and a small plan for it (strength training twice
  a week first, "after my morning coffee, 10 minutes of squats, push-ups and carries"), never with the
  later years alone. Later years are described gently ("often lean a little more on the people around
  you"), as a fading band, never as an age.
- "Could", not "should" (§1i). No guilt, no streaks that break; warm welcome-backs (§1e).
- Show the science behind a claim one tap away (already done with source links).
- The barrier-busting facts go where the hesitation is: next to Message (§1h).

### "It's a good time to…" (context-aware, later)
In the native app, with permission and all on-device:
- After they've been still for an hour (Apple Health / Health Connect): "a 5-minute walk?"
- After a meal time they set: "walk after dinner?"
- A free evening on their calendar: "dinner with Ana?"

This is a *just-in-time adaptive intervention* — like HeartSteps — and the strongest version of nudging, but
it needs the native build and careful limits so it never feels like surveillance.

### What we won't do
Daily nags, streak shame, leaderboards, guilt, dark patterns, or tracking people's behavior on a server.
